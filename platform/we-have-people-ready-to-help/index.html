Open Government and Civic Participation

We Have People Ready to Help. Let's Let Them.

A recent editorial lamented the cancellation of Cumberland's Citizen's Academy because only five people signed up.

The contrast is useful. Allegany County promotes its more than 30 boards and commissions as a way for residents to "help shape Allegany County," but the public often cannot see when many of them meet, what they discuss, what they recommend or what happens to their advice.

I do not believe people have stopped caring about their community. I think many have stopped believing that their time, ideas and involvement will make a difference.

The central idea

Volunteer boards and commissions should bring needed expertise, public experience and independent thinking into county government.

Public confidence

Why people lose confidence in the system

When I talk with people who want to become more involved in local government, I often suggest applying for a county board or commission. The answers I hear tell us a great deal about the public's confidence in the current system.

  • Questions about appointments

    “They only appoint people they are tight with.”

    “I applied, but I never heard anything.”

  • Concerns about independence

    “The commissioners and staff do not listen to the boards anyway.”

    “I was asked to serve, but it felt like I was expected to support the goals of the people who appointed me, rather than offer my independent opinion.”

  • Uncertainty about meetings

    “I served on a board, but it rarely met. When it did, I did not receive enough notice to be there.”

Whether every perception is completely accurate almost misses the point. When that is how people experience the system, or how they believe it operates, we have a problem.

The value of public service

An important resource already in our community

Volunteer boards and commissions are not supposed to be honorary clubs, political rewards or names on a county webpage. They are supposed to bring needed expertise, public experience and independent thinking into county government.

Allegany County cannot afford to keep every kind of professional expert on staff. But many of those people live right here. They are willing to give us a few hours each month, read reports, ask questions and share professional knowledge. That is a tremendous public resource, especially in a county where every dollar and every good idea matters.

Two especially important examples are the Ethics Commission and the Animal Control Advisory Board. Both deal with matters that directly affect public confidence, public safety and the county's legal responsibilities. Yet for years, appointments were not made and meetings were not held. They were only "reconstituted," the county's carefully chosen word, after I used one of my five-minute public comment periods to make it public that neither was functioning.

This is not meant to overlook the many people who are already doing good work. The Allegany County Commission for Women is one example. Its members have continued working on behalf of women and families across the county. But even there, I have heard people say they do not always feel valued and that their opinions do not always seem welcome.

Asking people to volunteer is not enough. We have to respect the time, experience and independent judgment they bring with them.

Public access and accountability

Make the work of every board visible

As a county commissioner, I will work to rebuild our boards and commissions into a meaningful path for civic participation. That begins with some very basic commitments.

Current information for every board

Every county-appointed board and commission will have an up-to-date page on the county website. That page should identify its purpose, legal authority, membership, current vacancies, term lengths and staff contact.

Public meetings and records

Regular meeting dates, established far in advance, will be posted in one easy-to-find county calendar. Meeting notices and agendas will be posted in advance. Minutes will be posted promptly after meetings. Supporting documents should also be available whenever possible, so residents can understand the issues being considered.

Annual reports on results

At least once each year, the county should report what boards studied, what they recommended, what changed because of their work, where vacancies remain and which boards need more support.

For boards covered by Maryland's Open Meetings Act, advance notice, agendas and minutes are not favors to the public. They are legal requirements. Simply doing the minimum required by law would be an improvement, but higher goals will bring better results.

Appointments and service

Make participation open and meaningful

The appointment process must also be more open. Political loyalty should not be the test.

Open the appointment process

Applicants should receive confirmation that their applications were received. Vacancies and upcoming term expirations should be advertised widely. Selection should be based on relevant experience, community involvement, geographic representation and willingness to do the work.

Provide training and support

Members should receive training on their responsibilities, the Maryland Open Meetings Act, ethics requirements, county procedures and the specific work of their boards. If we ask residents to serve, we should give them the information and support they need to serve well.

Respect independent judgment

Board members should not be appointed to carry out the wishes of the commissioners or county staff. They should be encouraged to ask difficult questions, disagree when necessary and offer honest recommendations based on what they know. That independence is not disloyalty. It is the value of the system.

Listening to volunteer boards

Give independent voices a meaningful role

County staff should assist boards without controlling access to them. A member of one board told me, when I tried to suggest a possible topic for future discussion, that all communications from the public had to go through staff.

I understand why legal matters and pending decisions must follow formal procedures. But residents should be able to offer ideas for future discussion without county staff serving as a gatekeeper between the public and its volunteer boards.

Proposed action

Commissioners should regularly receive board reports and recommendations. When a board spends months studying an issue and makes a recommendation, that work should be presented publicly and receive a public response.

The answer may not always be yes. But it should never be silence.

Members should be able to point to accomplishments and say, “I helped make that happen.” That is how we make public service worthwhile. It is also how we begin rebuilding confidence in county government.

Today's board members are often tomorrow's community leaders. This is one of the ways we develop good leaders for the future.

Looking ahead

Make people's time matter

The cancellation of Cumberland's Citizen's Academy is sad, but it should not lead us to conclude that people do not care. It should cause us to ask whether government has done enough to make civic involvement accessible, welcoming and meaningful.

I believe Allegany County is full of people who want to help. They have useful experience, fresh ideas and a real stake in our future. Given the chance, they can make a real difference.

The county's job is not merely to invite them into the room.

“The County Commission's obligation is to open the door, listen when they speak and make sure their time matters.” — Kit Pepper Lescallette
