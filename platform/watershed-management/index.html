The value of our watershed

From an extractive economy to a restorative economy

Allegany County's history is a lesson in extractive economics. Coal, timber and other resources created jobs, but much of the wealth they created left the area a while back. We need to see how we can move toward a restorative economy, one that protects its assets while creating lasting local value.

One of our greatest assets is our location within the Potomac River watershed. Yet we see watershed protection as a local expense we cannot afford. Georges Creek flows into the North Branch of the Potomac. Jennings Randolph Reservoir serves the Washington metropolitan region's water-supply system, and more than six million people live within the Potomac Basin. Conditions here matter far beyond Allegany County.

And here we are struggling to repair damage from the May 2025 flood and catch up on deferred maintenance. That damaged sewage pipe over Georges Creek may cost between $300,000 and $400,000 to repair permanently. Why should one damaged pipe potentially threaten people between here and Washington simply because our county cannot readily fund the repair?

The proper management of Allegany County's unique watershed matters to millions of people. We should recognize the value of that resource and develop partnerships that help pay for protecting it. That is part of moving away from extractive economic solutions that leave us poorer and poorer.

Understanding flood risks

Understand where the water goes

Pre-flood planning begins at the land-use level. A comprehensive watershed study can help us understand where water travels, which areas are most vulnerable, and how to reduce the economic impact of future floods.

Map flood risks

A countywide study must include land prioritization and flood-risk mapping across the watershed. It should show where water travels, how quickly it moves, which areas are most critical to flood resiliency and what infrastructure is at risk.

Examine land disturbances

Land disturbances on our mountains, including those we encouraged to happen, must be part of the discussion. Clearing, grading, deforestation and multiple development projects should be considered together when evaluating runoff and drainage.

Prioritize infrastructure

Identify vulnerable infrastructure and deferred maintenance. Use the results to help emergency planners predict which areas may be hit hardest and determine what investments could reduce damage from the next major storm.

Examining past development

Questions about Dans Mountain

The Dans Mountain wind project was approved after years of controversy over clearing and drainage. A hydrologic analysis warned that development could increase runoff and flooding risks for Braddock Run and Georges Creek. I believe those concerns were not given the consideration they deserved during the approval process.

I cannot say for sure that changes to the ridge for the windmills made the May 2025 flood worse. But the county cannot say for sure that they did not. And that is a problem.

Did stormwater facilities overflow or fail, as they were predicted to by the hydrologist? Were holding ponds damaged? Is anyone studying those questions and working with the windmill company?

We need answers before the next storm, not afterward.

New development and drainage

Understanding the impact of solar projects

Jade Meadow III is a 300-megawatt solar project approved by the Maryland Public Service Commission. The project site is primarily in Garrett County, with six Allegany County parcels identified for easement purposes, including a possible overhead collector line.

The overall project site covers approximately 5,933 acres, but the proposed limit of disturbance is approximately 1,852 acres across the entire project. Those figures should not be confused with the amount of land that would actually be disturbed within Allegany County.

Most of the development is on land previously used for coal mining. While most of the project is outside Allegany County, we still need to understand whether changes to drainage, grading, roads and other infrastructure could affect our watershed. Water does not stop at county boundaries, and watershed planning should consider the cumulative effects of development across the region.

Additional solar proposals are moving through the process in the Rawlings area. Maryland's 2025 Renewable Energy Certainty Act changed the Public Service Commission's review of renewable-energy projects. Local concerns involving watersheds, drainage and impervious surfaces need careful consideration as these projects move forward.

This is not about opposing solar or wind energy. It is about understanding where the water will go before approving more major land disturbances.

A proposed 90-day pause

Pause major land disturbances and begin planning

We should not wait for another major flood before examining how our land-use decisions affect the watershed.

Proposed action

I am asking for an emergency 90-day pause on county permits, approvals and recommendations for projects involving substantial clearing, grading or drainage changes in elevated and mountainous areas.

Review county land-use rules

During those 90 days, update the county's solar regulations and enforce the special-permitting provisions already in our code. Examine how county permitting decisions can better address clearing, grading and drainage changes.

Begin a watershed and drainage study

Start planning a comprehensive countywide study that considers the cumulative effects of multiple projects. Establish baseline information, map flood risks and identify drainage patterns and vulnerable infrastructure.

Develop partnerships

Identify our infrastructure needs and build partnerships with Maryland, federal agencies, the Army Corps of Engineers, the Interstate Commission on the Potomac River Basin and downstream water utilities.

A long-term approach

Protecting a resource that matters beyond Allegany County

On September 16, 2026, President Trump issued an executive order emphasizing objective measurements, proven water-management practices and measurable water-quality improvements. It also directs the Environmental Protection Agency to examine state and local stormwater fees and encourage their repeal. That makes the need for baseline data and measurable progress even more important.

This is not a request for someone to rescue us without a plan. We should produce the data, identify the risks and develop projects that protect our communities while also protecting water relied upon beyond Allegany County.

As we reduce our vulnerability to flooding, we can also make Allegany County more attractive to businesses and people looking to invest here.

That is how we begin moving from an extractive economy to a restorative economy: recognize the value of our assets, protect them and demonstrate that investment here creates benefits locally and downstream.

Please initiate the 90-day pause and begin the work needed to secure a countywide watershed study and infrastructure plan. It could be the most effective economic development decision you have made.
